A Former WrestlerWins the Leica Oskar Barnack Award with Dagestan

The 2026 LOBA goes to Damir Faizulin for “Preserving Nature as Preserving Ourselves,” a series he made while living in the Caucasus highlands as tourism pours in.

5ft.mag Editors 2026.10.13

This article was translated from Korean with AI. Read the original in Korean →

This year’s Leica Oskar Barnack Award (LOBA) has gone to Russian photographer Damir Faizulin. The winning work is “Preserving Nature as Preserving Ourselves,” a series shot in the mountain regions of Dagestan in the North Caucasus. Leica announced the winner on October 8 in Wetzlar, Germany. The prize is €40,000, along with €10,000 worth of Leica equipment.

An Award About People and Their Environment

LOBA has been awarded since 1980. It is named after Oskar Barnack, who created the Leica camera, and it goes to a photographer whose outstanding series captures the relationship between people and the environment they live in. Entry is by nomination, not open call. This year more than 130 photography experts from around the world nominated over 400 entries, and 12 finalists were announced in August. This is the 46th edition.

In the Ring Until 33

Faizulin’s background is unusual for a photography prize winner. He was born in 1986 in Uzbekistan and moved to Russia with his family after the Soviet Union collapsed. Until he was 33, he made his living as a martial artist and professional wrestler. Photography came after that. He learned it in Kaliningrad, attending workshops with documentary photographer Dmitry Markov.

He first went to Dagestan as a traveler in 2020. The following year he moved there for good. He has said he sensed the region was about to change quickly, and that this was why he started the project.

Tourism, New Buildings, and What Gets Left Behind

“Preserving Nature as Preserving Ourselves” follows the mountain villages of Dagestan as they change. Tourists arrive in greater numbers, and new buildings go up to receive them. Meanwhile, old houses and customs are left untended. The photographs show the landscape changing, and with it, traditions and cultural identity.

Still, the series does more than condemn. Faizulin records what is changing without stripping the land of its beauty. Moments when the balance between people and landscape breaks sit side by side with moments when it still holds. That balance is what the title speaks to: preserving nature means preserving ourselves.

The Newcomer Award and the First LOBA Women Grant

The Newcomer award went to American photographer Laila AnnMarie Stevens. Her winning work, “Clayton Sisterhood Project,” is a black-and-white series about her two older sisters and four nieces and nephews, who left Queens, New York, and made a home together on a single plot of land in Clayton, North Carolina. Stevens began working for The New York Times at 20 and studied photography and media at the Fashion Institute of Technology (FIT) in New York. She receives €10,000 and a Leica Q3.

The LOBA Women Grant, new this year, went to Italian photographer Valentina Sinis. Her ongoing project in Iraqi Kurdistan follows Kurdish women who left their homes to join the armed resistance. She receives €10,000, a Leica Q3, and professional support to help her complete the work.

What kind of answer suits an award that asks about people and their environment? A former wrestler went to live in an unfamiliar place and watched it change up close. This year’s winning work is the answer of someone who actually lived on that land.
