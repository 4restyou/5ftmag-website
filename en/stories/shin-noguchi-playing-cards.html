GOODS PLAYING CARDS

A Shin Noguchi Photo Book for Your PocketFulton's Playing Cards × Shin Noguchi

One photograph per card, with almost no cropping. It comes in three editions: the Fuji Box, the Oni Box, and the Gilded Oni Box.

5ft.mag Editors 2026.10.05

This article was translated from Korean with AI. Read the original in Korean →

"A deck that would feel like carrying a Shin Noguchi photo book with you anytime, anywhere."

That was the idea Fulton's Playing Cards, an American playing card company, brought to Shin Noguchi. The deck that came out of it was released on September 16. Every card carries one of his photographs.

Fulton's makes designer playing cards. It has released decks with Mickey Mouse, NASA, the film The Texas Chainsaw Massacre, and Monster Cereals. This time it filled an entire deck with the work of one photographer.

Shin Noguchi is a familiar name to 5ft.mag readers. Born in Tokyo, he is a street photographer who has spent nearly 20 years photographing the streets of Kamakura and Tokyo. He shoots chance moments in color, and he presses the shutter while meeting his subject's eyes. We ran an interview with him in Vol.02.

Related Shin Noguchi Interview: Finding the Extraordinary in Everyday Streets Shin Noguchi, who has photographed the streets of Kamakura and Tokyo for nearly 20 years, on how he looks. The opening of our Vol.02 interview.

Almost no cropping

The first thing Noguchi points to is the crop. Each photograph went onto its card almost uncut. That is why he calls the deck "a small photo book that you can carry around in your pocket."

The product shots show how it works. Horizontal photographs are printed so you turn the card sideways to see them, and vertical ones stay upright. The ranks and suits sit small inside the white border, clear of the image. A playing card is a tall format. Instead of cutting the photographs to fit it, the deck turns the card to fit the photograph.

There is no set way to play. You can simply flip through the photographs, or use the cards as an ordinary deck. Noguchi suggests you might even come up with your own games, such as matching the subjects or scenes that appear in the photographs.

Anyone who shoots film will stop at this one. The border of the lower card reproduces the edge numbers: the film name and frame numbers printed along the rebate outside the perforations, which appear once the film is developed. The Leica and the Portra 400 canister placed beside the deck in every product shot tell the same story.

The photographs in the deck

The deck includes several of his best-known pictures.

What changes when street photographs become playing cards? A photo book is read front to back. Cards get shuffled and passed from hand to hand. The order changes with every game, and you meet the pictures without the sequence the photographer set.

Three designs, three prices

  • Fuji Box Standard edition, with Mount Fuji on the box. $25
  • Oni Box Limited edition, showing an oni playing with the cards. $35
  • Gilded Oni Box Limited edition with metallic finishing on the card edges. Several colors, each in limited quantities. $52

Prices are in US dollars. Check the Fulton's checkout page for shipping to your country and its cost.

A signed edition, Japan only

Noguchi is also selling standard Fuji Boxes from his own stock with his signature. He signed two places: the outer box and the photographer card inside. The Signed Fuji Box is sold only through his online store for Japan, while stock lasts.

One for home, one for school

Near the end of his announcement, Noguchi writes:

One for your home. One for your school, facility, or office. Or, why not one for every public space?

His photographs have run in magazines and newspapers through commissions. They now also turn up where people meet them in daily life: in advertising, on film festival posters, on record covers. He says that makes him happy, and the deck is the newest place on that list. He closes by saying he hopes to share the beauty he finds in everyday life in Japan with as many people as possible.

Pictures that would have stood on a bookshelf as a photo book are spread across kitchen tables and floors.
