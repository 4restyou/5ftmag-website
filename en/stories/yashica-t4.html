Yashica T4A Carl Zeiss Lens That Fits in Your Pocket

The camera Ryan McGinley took out every night before anyone knew his name. How did a 1990s automatic point-and-shoot become one of the most expensive point-and-shoots on the film camera market?

5ft.mag Editors 2026.10.17

This article was translated from Korean with AI. Read the original in Korean →

From 1999 to 2003, a young photographer in New York went out almost every night with ten rolls of film and a small camera in his pockets. That was Ryan McGinley. Most of the pictures that took him all the way to a solo show at the Whitney Museum came out of that camera. It was a Yashica T4.

A Point-and-Shoot from Kyocera and Carl Zeiss

Yashica started out as a Japanese camera company. In 1983 Kyocera bought the struggling Yashica, and Kyocera also ran the Contax brand in partnership with Carl Zeiss. The T series of automatic cameras came out of that partnership. The T4 arrived in the early 1990s. Sources differ on the exact year, giving either 1990 or 1992.

What matters most on the T4 is the lens: a multicoated Carl Zeiss Tessar 35mm f/3.5 with four elements in three groups. Tessar is the name of a classic lens design that Carl Zeiss has used for a very long time. It is sharp in the center of the frame with crisp contrast, and many reviewers say it produces results you would not expect from such a small camera.

What It Can and Can't Do

Using it is simple. Turn it on, frame the shot, press the shutter. The camera sets focus and exposure. It chooses a shutter speed on its own anywhere from 1 s to 1/700 s, and the closest focusing distance is 35 cm. It reads film speed from the DX code, from ISO 50 to 3200. Film with no DX code, or outside that range, is set to ISO 100. It runs on a single CR123A battery and weighs a little over 170 g.

The T4 Super that followed (also called the T5 in some markets) added two things: weather resistance, and a small “Super Scope” finder on the top of the camera. You could look down into it and frame from waist height, which made it good for shooting discreetly below people's eye level.

Size was also what led McGinley to this camera. He said he bought one after reading a piece in the skateboarding magazine Big Brother in which the photographer Earl Parker praised the T4. It fit in his pocket, so he could take it out every night. He shot ISO 1600 film under the fluorescent lights of bars and used the self-timer to photograph himself. He reportedly pulled it out even in spots where water was splashing.

Why Is It So Expensive Now?

The T4 was once a common secondhand camera. How did a single point-and-shoot get this expensive? Reviews published abroad in 2025 put a T4 in good condition at $500 to $600. Everyone agrees the lens is still good. But many say the price owes less to performance than to the story of famous photographers using it and to trends on social media.

So if you buy a T4 now, check a few things first. Point-and-shoots are hard to repair. Make sure it powers on, that the lens cover opens and closes properly, that the flash fires, and that the LCD display still works. For the same money, it is also worth comparing other prime-lens point-and-shoots from the same period.

What the T4 left behind may be less the camera itself than a way of shooting. A camera small enough to carry in your pocket at all times, ready to shoot the moment you take it out. That is also why McGinley's early photographs still feel so alive.

Info

  • Camera Yashica T4 (made by Kyocera), released in the early 1990s. Successor: T4 Super (T5)
  • Lens Carl Zeiss Tessar 35mm f/3.5, four elements in three groups, multicoated
  • Shutter and focus Automatic, 1 s to 1/700 s; autofocus; minimum focusing distance 0.35 m
  • Film and power 35mm (135) film, DX ISO 50 to 3200, CR123A battery
