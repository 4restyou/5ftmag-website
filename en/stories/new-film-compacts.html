CAMERA 2026 / 10

No more digging through used listingsSix new film compacts

From a $65 camera with the Kodak name on it to a premium compact that focuses with LiDAR. Film compacts are being made new again.

5ft.mag Editors 2026.10.19

This article was translated from Korean with AI. Read the original in Korean →

There was a time when the only way to buy a film compact was to dig through the used market. After the big camera makers walked away from film in the late 2000s, about the only new cameras left were disposables. Meanwhile, prices for 1990s compacts climbed steeply. A well-known model like the Yashica T4 in good condition sells for $500 to $600.

That has changed over the past few years. New film cameras are coming out again, from budget models carrying the Kodak name to premium autofocus compacts. Here are six of them, from cheapest to most expensive. Prices are launch prices, and what you pay in Korea may differ.

Kodak Ektar H35N, 72 shots from one roll

A half-frame camera that gets more than 70 shots out of a 36-exposure roll. The lens is 22mm and the shutter is fixed at 1/100s. Screw in a cable release and you can shoot bulb, holding the shutter open. The flash runs on a single AAA battery, and there is a built-in star filter that splits points of light into star shapes. At $64.99, it is the cheapest of the six.

One catch: some labs will not scan or print half-frame, so ask before you shoot. You also end up with twice as many prints.

Kodak Snapic A1, a $99 automatic compact

It has a 25mm f/9.5 lens built from three glass elements, with two-step focus: one setting for near, one for far. The shutter is 1/100s and exposure is automatic. Two AAA batteries power the flash, and the camera weighs about 120g. Some reviews say the flash does not reach more than a few steps indoors. It comes in gray or white.

FilmNeverDie Nana, a metal camera made by a lab

This one was built in-house by FilmNeverDie, a film lab in Australia. The body is metal, and the film advances on its own after every shot. The 31mm lens is fixed-focus, sharp from 1.5m to infinity. It takes AA batteries and weighs 300g. It started on Kickstarter, went on general sale in November 2024, and costs around $230. What stands out is who made it: people who handle film every day.

Pentax 17, the first new film camera in 20 years

Pentax released this half-frame camera in June 2024, its first new film camera in about 20 years. The 25mm f/3.5 lens focuses by turning through six zones, and you advance the film by hand with a lever. It launched in Europe at €579. Brisnap TV wrote about using one in "Pentax 17 is a perfectly good first film camera."

Lomo MC-A, Lomography's first autofocus camera

Lomography announced its first 35mm autofocus camera in October 2025. The 32mm f/2.8 lens focuses with LiDAR ranging, and you can choose anything from program auto to full manual. Shutter speeds run from bulb to 1/500s, and it can stack several exposures on one frame. It costs $549. We covered the film advance problems in early units, and whether it is worth buying now, in "When film focuses itself: Lomo MC-A."

Rollei 35AF, a premium compact that focuses with LiDAR

MiNT released it under the Rollei name in 2024. It borrows the look of the 1960s Rollei 35, but everything inside is new. The 35mm f/2.8 lens focuses with a LiDAR sensor, and exposure switches between auto and manual. There is a built-in flash, and you advance and rewind the film by hand. It launched in the UK from £762 for the silver version, the most expensive of the six.

One that has not shipped yet

There is also the aF-1, unveiled in January 2025 by Analogue of Amsterdam, the Netherlands. It has a 35mm f/2.8 lens, LiDAR autofocus, a shutter that reaches 1/1000s and motorized film advance, and it was announced at €399. Shipping was first promised for summer 2025, but shutter durability issues and prototype revisions pushed it back. As of August this year, there was still no ship date.

Which one to choose

Line the six up and the price gap is more than tenfold. If you are new to film, either Kodak will do. Their lenses are slow and they have only one shutter speed, so they work best outdoors on a sunny day. If you want a roll to last, go half-frame with the H35N or the Pentax 17. If you want to set focus and exposure yourself, compare the Pentax 17 and the Lomo MC-A. If you need autofocus, compare the Lomo MC-A and the Rollei 35AF.

A new camera comes with one thing a used one does not: somewhere to fix it when it breaks. Parts for 1990s compacts have dried up, and many are hard to repair. So between a used T4 at over $500 and a brand-new MC-A at $549, which one will stay with you longer?
