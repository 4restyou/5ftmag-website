She took the pictures and never showed them

Vivian Maier, the nanny who looked after other people's children all her life and took 150,000 photographs

Film Social Club 2026.06.27

This article was translated from Korean with AI. Read the original in Korean →

Shoot film for a while and at some point a strange thought crosses your mind. What if I never showed my film to anyone, never even developed it, and just kept it piled up in boxes for the rest of my life? Vivian Maier actually lived that thought.

Chicago, 2007, a storage locker auction. Belongings seized over unpaid storage fees were going for next to nothing, and a young man named John Maloof bought a box full of negatives for about 380 dollars. He was hoping to find old pictures for a local history book. Neither the man who bought the box nor the woman who shot what was inside knew it held a chapter of 20th-century street photography.

Her day job was nanny

Vivian Maier (1926–2009) was not a photographer. To be precise, no one called her a photographer while she was alive. She worked as a nanny. Born in New York, she spent part of her childhood in France, came back to the US and looked after children in families on Chicago's North Shore for close to 40 years. On her days off, she slung a camera over her shoulder and went out into the streets. That was it. No exhibitions, no prints, no showing anyone.

A camera at the waist

Her main camera was a Rolleiflex. You don't raise it to your eye and aim. You hold it at your waist and focus looking down into it. That posture has a curious effect: people don't realize they are being photographed, because the lens isn't pointed straight at their face. Part of the reason Maier could get so close to faces on the street, and catch them so unguarded, was the shape of this square 6x6 camera.

She left many self-portraits too, slipping her reflection into a mirror or a shop window. Not the kind of self-portrait that says "look at me," but one that hides her in a corner of a street scene. Fitting for someone who never put herself forward.

The streets she saw

Maier's streets have no hierarchy. An old woman wrapped in fur, a boy leading a horse under the elevated tracks, a kid with folded arms in front of a glove shop: all are taken at the same eye level. Maybe because she lived as a nanny, her shutter lingers especially on children. The look on a kid with something smeared on his face, arms crossed like a grown-up, is the kind of picture only someone who has spent a long time watching children can catch.

Not everything was perfect, either. She kept shaky, smeared frames as they were. One shot of Audrey Hepburn passing through a crowd is out of focus and blurred, yet the chance of that moment feels all the more alive for it.

Film she never developed

This is the part that stays longest with anyone who shoots film. Much of the film Maier left behind was never even developed in her lifetime. Shoot, rewind, put it in a box, and that was the end of it. Film has already taken in the light the moment you press the shutter. The image just stays invisible until it goes through the developer. Maier's film slept in its boxes for decades in that invisible state.

Why didn't she develop it? No money, no time, or maybe the act of taking the picture was already enough. Nobody knows for sure. What is clear is that photography, for her, was not a result to show other people. It was the act of seeing itself.

When the world noticed, she was gone

When Maloof scanned the negatives and posted them on a blog, the response exploded. Exhibitions opened, photobooks came out, and in 2013 the documentary 'Finding Vivian Maier' was made and went on to an Academy Award nomination. Her pictures hung on walls for the first time, and people stopped in front of them.

But Maier herself saw none of it. She died quietly, in poverty, in 2009, just as her film was starting to become known. After her death came a long fight over the copyright. Who would handle the work of someone who never sold a single print in her life, and how? Everyone had a say except her.

"Good pictures get recognized eventually." In front of Maier, you want to believe that, at least once. But that "eventually" came only after she was gone, and what brought her out into the world was a storage auction and a scanner, not a critic. And all of it was possible because film held on to the light of those days, intact, for decades. Shoot it and you can always take it out and look again someday. That quiet promise we film shooters lean on, Vivian Maier proved with 150,000 frames.
